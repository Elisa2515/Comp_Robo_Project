## Reflection: From Robot Behaviors to Robot Intelligence
Through the robot behaviors project and the robot localization exercise, I developed a much deeper understanding of how mechanical systems and software intelligence come together to create functional robots. Before these projects, I tended to think of robotics as divided into separate domains; mechanical design, electronics, and programming. Working with the simulated Neato worlds helped me see that these components are inseparable. The mechanical form of the robot directly affects how it senses, moves, and makes decisions, and the software “brain” must be designed with those physical constraints in mind.

In the robot behaviors project, I focused on implementing foundational behaviors such as driving in a square, wall following, person following, with some obstacle avoidance. These behaviors introduced me to the idea of reactive control, where the robot continuously responds to sensor inputs rather than executing a fixed script. This project helped me develop intuition about how software decisions manifest physically. For example, when tuning wall-following behavior, I could immediately see how changes in sensor processing affected the robot’s path relative to the wall in the simulated world. This feedback loop between code and physical motion strengthened my understanding of control systems and reinforced the importance of testing and iteration. As a mechanical engineering student, this was especially valuable because it connected abstract control concepts to tangible motion and geometry.

The robot localization exercise built directly on this foundation by introducing probabilistic reasoning. Instead of assuming the robot always knows where it is, I learned how localization algorithms explicitly represent uncertainty. Implementing a simplified particle filter showed me how a robot can maintain multiple hypotheses about its position and gradually converge on a likely pose as it moves. Even without using a full map-based sensor model, the exercise clarified the roles of motion uncertainty, noise, weighting, and resampling in state estimation.

What stood out most to me in the localization exercise was how visualization transformed my understanding. Seeing a cloud of particles spread, shift, and collapse in RViz made the abstract idea of probability distributions feel concrete. It became clear why robotics relies so heavily on visualization tools: probabilistic systems are difficult to debug without being able to “see” uncertainty evolve over time. Together, these two projects helped me see how robot behaviors and localization can serve as the foundation for building more complex robotic systems. Behaviors give a robot the ability to interact with its environment, while localization gives it context, an internal understanding of where it is and how it is moving. Combining these capabilities is what allows a robot to act intentionally rather than react blindly.

As a MechE major these projects helped me envision how I could design my own robot, such as a Neato-inspired platform with added legs or arms. I would feel confident that I could give it a functioning “brain.” Understanding how behaviors like wall following and person following are implemented makes it easier to design mechanical features that support those behaviors, such as sensor placement, actuator selection, and structural layout. Most importantly, these projects increased my confidence in working at the intersection of hardware and software. I now feel capable of designing a mechanical system and also writing the control and estimation code that allows it to think.
